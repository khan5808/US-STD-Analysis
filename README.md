# What U.S. STD Data Shows About Trends, Risks, and Gaps
States with the largest populations tend to report the highest numbers of STD cases, but the story is more complex when you look at rates per 100,000 people and break things down by disease type and gender. I set out to explore these patterns using more than 30 datasets from public sources, aiming to help guide better decisions in public health and prevention.

## Why I Chose This Project
Sexually transmitted diseases affect millions each year, but the way they show up across the U.S. isn’t uniform. I wanted to see what the numbers actually say about which diseases are most common, how trends differ by state and gender, and where prevention efforts might have the most impact. By digging into the real data, I hoped to highlight both clear patterns and areas that need more attention.

## The Data and How I Worked With It
My analysis used over 30 datasets, totaling more than 7,000 records, with information on age, gender, specific STD indicators, year, population, rates, and raw case counts. The data came mostly from government sites and research think tanks. I cleaned and explored the data using Python (mainly with pandas), making sure numbers were consistent and ready for comparison across different sources.

## What I Found in the Data
The clearest pattern was that states with bigger populations had the highest total STD case counts. But when adjusting for population size—using rates per 100,000 people—some less-populated states showed higher rates for certain diseases. This suggests that looking only at total numbers can miss where the risk is actually highest.

Chlamydia and gonorrhea stood out as the most commonly reported STDs in both case counts and rates, with syphilis following behind. Visualizations comparing case counts and rates over time made it clear that these two infections continue to drive the majority of the reported disease burden.

Gender patterns also showed up in the data. In some years, females had higher testing volumes, but infection rates varied by disease and period. This difference in testing and reporting could affect how we interpret trends by gender.

Geographic maps and charts highlighted differences between states. While large states like California and Texas had the most cases, some smaller states had higher rates per 100,000 people, which could signal local outbreaks or differences in reporting.

I also looked at state-level demographics, such as education and household factors, alongside STD rates. While visualizations showed some possible relationships, there wasn’t enough evidence to say these factors cause higher or lower rates. Still, these patterns could be worth exploring in future studies.

## Why This Matters
Understanding both the raw counts and the rates per population helps public health workers and policymakers target interventions more effectively. Focusing on rates, not just totals, can reveal hidden hotspots. Differences by gender and disease type also show that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be enough.

## What’s Next?
A good next step would be to dig deeper into the connections between STD rates and socioeconomic factors, or to track the impact of specific prevention programs over time. More granular data, especially at the local or community level, could help answer questions that state-level data alone can’t resolve.
